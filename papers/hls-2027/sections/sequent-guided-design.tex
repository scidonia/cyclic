\section{How the Sequent Calculus Guided Design}
\label{sec:design}

The extensions did not arise from intuition about supercompilation.  They
arose from asking a single precise proof-theoretic question: \emph{what is
generalisation, exactly?}  This question yielded two productive answers and
one precise negative result.

\subsection{Generalisation is cut introduction}

In the sequent calculus, cut elimination is mechanical (reduce until no more
cuts remain).  Cut introduction is creative: it means inventing a formula $C$
such that the proof splits into ``prove $C$'' and ``use $C$ to prove the goal.''
Generalisation in SC is exactly this: invent a configuration $S_{\mathsf{gen}}$
such that both the current and companion configurations are instances of it.

Standard anti-unification is one algorithm for cut introduction, but it is
\emph{purely syntactic}: it finds the most specific common generalisation of
two terms as terms.  It cannot discover:

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Fusion identities}: $\map\ f\ (\map\ g\ l)$ and $\map\ (f \circ g)\ l$
  are syntactically unrelated, but semantically equal.  Anti-unification
  produces $\map\ ?_0\ ?_1$ --- a trivial generalisation that enables no folding.
\item \textbf{Accumulator patterns}: $\mathsf{sum}\ (\mathsf{rev}\ l)$ does not
  share syntactic structure with $\mathsf{sum\_acc}\ l\ 0$, but the latter is
  the accumulator form that enables a tail-recursive residual.
\end{itemize}

Once we identified generalisation as cut introduction, the solution became
clear: replace the syntactic heuristic with an \emph{oracle} that can propose
cuts with semantic content.  The oracle need not be trusted --- the kernel
check (trace condition) is the soundness backstop.

\subsection{Speculation: a negative result}

A third candidate extension was uncovered by the same methodology.  Eisner and
Blatz describe \emph{speculation}: ``if a subcomputer of some rule is independent
of certain variables, hoist it out.''  Could this be a generalisation step?
The sequent calculus reveals the answer is no: speculation corresponds to
\emph{projection} (dropping context variables), not \emph{abstraction}
(extending context with fresh holes).  

The proof (Section~\ref{sec:speculation}) proceeds by: (a)~implementing
speculation as a generalisation step (\texttt{SpeculationGen.v}), (b)~proving
a roundtrip theorem that the substitution reconstructs the subterm, and
(c)~observing that for the fold to be CIU-correct, the subterm must be
the \emph{whole} term --- making speculation redundant with anti-unification
when it is sound.  The result is a precise characterisation: in canonical
configurations, speculation as a generalisation step is either redundant or
unsound.  Projection requires a \emph{splitting} step (different proof-theoretic
operation), not a generalisation step.

The negative result itself is a product of the sequent formalism.  Without
the vocabulary of context morphisms and cut formulas, the boundary between
anti-unification and projection would remain blurred.

\subsection{The architecture that follows}

Both productive gaps point to the same architectural pattern: extend the
generalisation step with new layers that run \emph{after} anti-unification
fails:

The CIU theorem is indifferent to which layer produced the generalisation:
only the trace check matters.  This modularity is a consequence of the
sequent formulation, where soundness is at the level of the proof
\emph{graph}, not the construction \emph{procedure}.  The speculation
negative result (Section~\ref{sec:speculation}) confirms that the
design space is not arbitrary --- the proof theory distinguishes
productive extensions from dead ends.
